# PRD Quality Review — Banhall step-by-step PD generation: idea seeds before prose

## Overall verdict
The PRD has a clear product thesis, concrete writer interactions and unusually useful brownfield guardrails; it provides a strong basis for UX exploration. It needs targeted revision before architecture and epics treat it as an implementation contract: stale suggestions can bypass review, the call cap can strand an unfinished plan, and post-sign-off changes lack a defined lifecycle. Grounding and advancement-mapping rules also need tightening so the shorter planning flow preserves the intended factual discipline.

## Decision-readiness — adequate
The main choice is explicit: §1 moves positioning decisions ahead of prose, §7 excludes another prose-approval loop, and addendum §A records alternatives with actual disadvantages, including the cost of retaining two gates. §10 names the inherited contracts being amended instead of concealing the additional interaction and call budget. These are useful decisions for a small writing team.

The seven Open Questions in §11 mostly have actionable defaults, and the draft openly says it was produced unattended. Work-plan optionality and production latency can reasonably remain provisional during UX and architecture. The unresolved runtime behavior identified below is more consequential than those acknowledged questions: downstream teams would have to make product decisions to close it.

## Substance over theater — strong
The vision is specific to Banhall: §1 contrasts deciding project positioning with hunting through a “50-line” section, and ties that pain to Larry's revision experience. One named writer carries four scenarios that exercise selection, feedback, changing an upstream decision and final drafting; there is no persona padding or unsupported innovation claim.

The NFRs address this feature's actual pressures: NFR-2 attributes seed calls, NFR-4 constrains client-derived prompt inputs, and NFR-6 bounds growing seed histories. Numerical latency and call thresholds are explicitly unmeasured assumptions, supported by an appropriately provisional cost model in addendum §C. Their presence is useful even though the hard-cap behavior needs correction.

## Strategic coherence — adequate
The features follow a coherent experience bet: short choices establish shared positioning, the Summary makes the whole plan reviewable, and the existing drafting path realizes it. SM-4 tests fewer corrections, while SM-C2 and SM-C4 counterbalance interaction cost and unsupported claims. The MVP's refusal to expand into chat integration, configurable templates or another orchestration framework keeps that arc intact.

One rule confuses the source of an advancement with a guaranteed advancement, and the time metric conflicts with the explicitly asynchronous working pattern. Both matter when deciding whether this flow actually improves writers' work.

### Findings
- **high** Experiment coverage is treated as advancement cardinality (§4.2 FR-8; §4.3 FR-16; §10 item 7) — FR-8 requires “at least one Seed per Selection of 244 · Experimentation / Iterations,” while FR-16 makes each selected advancement a distinct prose item. The supplied `extract-prior-prd-spec.md` §4 describes the existing role as “specific advancements (one per resolved uncertainty).” Several experiments can address one uncertainty, and an experiment need not establish a separate advancement. The PRD claims the content-role amendment is “honoured” without resolving this semantic change. *Fix:* Require advancement suggestions to trace to selected experiments and their evidenced outcomes, allowing many experiments per advancement and no advancement where unsupported; explicitly record any intended change to the existing role contract.
- **medium** Sign-off time counts the interruptions the product promises to support (§3.1; §9 SM-2) — The contextual job is to “work a project in fragments across a day,” and UJ-3 resumes “next morning,” but SM-2 measures “time from generation start to Sign-off” against 30 minutes. That clock would label the intended usage pattern a failure even if the writer spent very little time working. *Fix:* Define active writer time, including treatment of AI waits and idle intervals, or restrict the threshold to observed uninterrupted sessions and track elapsed completion time separately.

## Done-ness clarity — thin
All 38 FRs have testable consequences. Seed size and tag validation, explicit access checks, persistent selections, summary readiness and report-creation boundaries are concrete enough to drive stories. The weakness is the interaction between those individually clear rules: several valid user paths have no consistent completion behavior.

### Findings
- **high** Previously generated suggestions can bypass dependency review (§4.1 FR-4; §4.4 FR-17–FR-19; §4.5 FR-20) — FR-17 excludes Subsections that are “in progress” because “their next Batch will simply use the new Selections,” but FR-4 reopens their existing Batch unchanged and FR-15 permits approval without requesting another Batch. A writer can open 246 first, change the uncertainties in 242, then approve the old 246 suggestions without any stale warning. Prefetched suggestions create the same exposure. *Fix:* Track the context version used by every displayed Batch and require visible reconfirmation or regeneration before approving a Batch whose upstream context changed, including late-arriving results and previously unapproved Batches.
- **high** The hard cap can make sign-off unreachable (§5 NFR-3; §4.5 FR-20) — At 60 calls the stage “refuses further Batches,” supposedly “with cancel or sign-off still available.” A writer can spend the allowance on early Subsections while mandatory ones remain untouched, so FR-20 forbids sign-off. The stated recovery then reduces to abandoning the generation. It is also unclear whether the cap blocks Feedback and validation retries, since only further Batches are named. *Fix:* Define one call-accounting rule and a completion path at the cap, such as reserving first-pass capacity plus an explicit bounded recovery allowance, or allowing an authorized budget extension while retaining the plan.
- **high** Post-sign-off editing introduces an unspecified second lifecycle (§4.5 FR-23; §4.6 FR-27–FR-28) — FR-23 says “Any Selection change after sign-off creates a new Summary version and offers ‘Regenerate from summary’,” but FR-28 makes the post-generation Summary read-only and no route back to editable selections is defined. The requirements do not say whether changes are allowed during drafting, whether a revised plan must clear staleness and be signed off again, or whether regeneration creates another report or replaces an edited one. *Fix:* Either explicitly defer post-sign-off plan revision, or specify its entry point, renewed approval rules, active-generation behavior and report destination while preserving the original signed-off version and existing prose-write authority.
- **high** Editing a supported Seed can leave its changed claim looking source-backed (§4.2 FR-7; §4.3 FR-11; §4.5 FR-21) — FR-7 classifies Seeds through matching source excerpts and reserves “writer-asserted” for Unsupported Selections. FR-11 lets the writer replace the bullets while retaining only an “Edited” marker; it never states what happens to provenance or the assertion classification. An edited experiment result could therefore retain the old provenance chip even though its new claim is absent from that excerpt, and then enter the signed-off plan as supported. *Fix:* Specify how edited claims retain original-source history while distinguishing writer assertions from source-supported wording in cards, Summary and drafting input. Require explicit confirmation or revalidation when changed factual content is no longer supported by the cited excerpt.

## Scope honesty — adequate
§7 makes exclusions useful, especially retaining single/compare behavior and separating plan selection from report editing. Eight inline assumptions round-trip to seven index entries, and the three PM notes identify real trade-offs. For an internal draft feeding further design work, that density is proportionate; the default decisions are visible enough to revise.

One explicit handoff requirement disappeared without being adopted or deferred.

### Findings
- **medium** Reversibility stops short of the handoff's undo requirement (§4.3 FR-11–FR-14; §7–§8) — The handoff's Recommendation and Regeneration Context requires “support undo for destructive changes.” The PRD provides prior Batch restoration and reversible Skip, but Seed Edits only keep the original wording “viewable,” and Feedback supersedes and deselects the original without a stated restore action. Undo is neither a requirement nor a non-goal. *Fix:* State which changes can be undone and what restoration does to selections, approvals and staleness, or explicitly defer undo while naming the recoveries MVP does provide.

## Downstream usability — adequate
The stable FR IDs, fixed thirteen-role table, explicit vocabulary, testable consequences and separation of nonbinding mechanisms into the addendum are useful for UX, architecture and epics. FR-2 expressly preserves the three-section report contract, and §10 offers a concrete amendment list. Mechanical issues are minor compared with the behavioral findings above.

### Findings
- **medium** The feedback example promises a split that its contract does not explain (§3.3 UJ-2; §4.3 FR-12; §4.3 FR-16) — Larry asks to “split the caching experiment from the batching one,” but FR-12 returns “exactly one Seed” and FR-16 makes one Selection one distinct experiment. UJ-2 then moves from three correct Seeds and one conflated Seed to four selected iterations. A two-bullet revision could still be one Selection, leaving UX and story authors to decide whether the requested split actually occurred. *Fix:* Either change the example to a one-item correction, or explicitly support splitting into separately selectable Seeds and define selection inheritance and batch-size behavior.

## Shape fit — adequate
This is an appropriate capability-oriented PRD for a small writing team: the four journeys earn their space by explaining meaningful interactions, without inventing additional personas or a market-analysis wrapper. Brownfield constraints are mostly well handled: existing mode identity, authorization, report creation, legacy runs and shared content roles are preserved or explicitly addressed. One central current-state claim conflicts with the supplied implementation evidence and should be reconciled before it guides prioritization.

### Findings
- **medium** The current-stepper diagnosis treats reported experience as verified absence (§0; §1; addendum §A option A) — §0 says the existing flow offers approval “with no edit or feedback path,” and addendum option A proposes to “add edit + feedback.” However, `extract-codebase.md` §5 identifies a textarea edit and guidance input in `IterativeStepper.svelte`, and §3 identifies regeneration with free-text guidance. The source chat brief reports the absence, so this is a real discrepancy between writer experience and implementation evidence, not grounds to discard the problem. *Fix:* Distinguish the writer's reported experience from implemented capability, investigate visibility or deployed-version differences, and restate the demonstrated redesign benefit as choosing ideas before reviewing full-section prose.

## Mechanical notes
- FR-1 through FR-38, UJ-1 through UJ-4, SM-1 through SM-6 and SM-C1 through SM-C4 are unique and contiguous. Requirement references resolve within those ranges; inherited references are generally qualified with the prior PRD date.
- §0 says assumptions are “indexed in §9”; the Assumptions Index is §12. Correct that cross-reference.
- All eight concrete inline assumption tags are covered by the seven index bullets, with NFR-1 and NFR-3 deliberately combined. No orphan index entries found. There are seven Open Questions and three PM callouts.
- New glossary terms are largely consistent. Inherited terms are listed rather than defined locally; include their definitions in downstream source bundles, especially Corrections-to-acceptable, which SM-4 references but §2 does not list.
- Each UJ names Larry, but UJ-2's “same project,” UJ-3's “next morning,” and the opening sentence that supplies Larry's role depend on surrounding context. Repeat the relevant role/project context if exporting journeys individually.
- The UJs shorten several Subsection names, including “Overall improvements” in UJ-3. Bind extracted stories to FR-2's canonical names rather than treating those shortened labels as additional roles.
- The sections required for these stakes are present: vision, user context, journeys, FRs, NFRs, constraints, non-goals, MVP scope, metrics with counter-metrics, amendments, questions and assumptions. No additional template sections are needed.
